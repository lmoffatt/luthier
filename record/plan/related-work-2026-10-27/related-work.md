This document reviews the literature that a paper or grant proposal on Luthier must cite. It is organized around the thirteen lines of work named in `BRIEF.md`, followed by five additional lines of work that are closer to specific parts of Luthier's design, and concludes with a plain assessment of what is new in Luthier's combination and what is already established.

## 1. PetaBricks and Algorithmic Choice with Accuracy

### What it does
PetaBricks is a language and autotuning compiler in which algorithmic choice is a first-class construct. A programmer specifies multiple ways to solve a problem, including compositions of different algorithms at different recursion depths or data sizes, and the compiler empirically autotunes across choices to construct hybrid algorithms. Its variable-accuracy extension adds accuracy metrics and user-specified accuracy targets to the language so the compiler searches the Pareto frontier of time and accuracy, generating configurations configured for distinct accuracy demands. A recent large-scale empirical study by Gilot, Wrigstad, and Darulova (2026) complements this picture by showing that floating-point software in practice consists of modular, multi-function pipelines with control flow rather than isolated kernels.

DOI: [10.1145/1542476.1542481](https://doi.org/10.1145/1542476.1542481) `[verified]`

DOI: [10.1109/CGO.2011.5764677](https://doi.org/10.1109/CGO.2011.5764677) `[verified]`

### How it resembles Luthier
PetaBricks is the closest systems precedent to Luthier's principle `L6` ("one function, many specimens, chosen by the performance the demander chooses, along certified identities") and to the separation between the declarative program and the executive `Demand` (`L5.1`, spec 8 and 9). In both systems, an operation is not locked to a single implementation, accuracy is specified by the caller or demand rather than hard-coded inside the algorithm (`P11`), and a planner chooses a realization that meets the requested accuracy while optimizing resource usage.

### How it differs
In PetaBricks, the relationship between candidate algorithms is untyped and uncertified: the programmer lists alternatives in a `transform` header, and the autotuner measures accuracy empirically on training inputs without a semantic theory of what the operation means or when two implementations may be substituted. In Luthier, each candidate is a *specimen* certified as a model of a shared `Theory` (`G1`), and substitution is permitted only along a certified identity (`D-2026-10-11-m`, `L6.5`, `L6.6`). Because metric closeness is a tolerance relation and not transitive (`D-2026-10-18-c`, `concepts.md` sections 3.2 and 13), Luthier does not treat interchangeable algorithms as an equivalence class; instead, it models them as a graph of certified 2-cells whose error bounds and evidence costs accumulate along paths (`L10.7`) and pull back through bound transformers (`L6.8`).

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Hierarchical and poly-algorithmic choice by input region:** PetaBricks switches algorithms across input sizes (for example, switching matrix multiplication or sorting algorithms below a size cutoff). Luthier's realization graph and domain-indexed certificates (`(domain, cost)`, `L10.2`) can express poly-algorithms where one specimen is certified and fast on small inputs and another on large inputs.
2. **Pareto-frontier pruning for the planner:** PetaBricks prunes dominated candidate configurations during search. When Luthier builds its planner (`record/plan/audit-algebras-2026-10-22/execution.md` wave E), it can prune specimens that are dominated in all coordinates of `(bound, evidence cost, resources)` on a given domain before running backward pull-back search.
3. **Empirical scaling fits:** PetaBricks fits empirical cost and accuracy curves across input sizes, which directly informs bite C2 (`concepts.md` section 9.5, `L9.17`), where Luthier's tester checks how an algorithm's resource budget scales with the demanded bound.

## 2. Herbie and Daisy (Floating-Point Accuracy and Verification)

### What it does
Herbie and Daisy represent the two main branches of automated floating-point accuracy tooling in programming languages: empirical-guided rewriting and sound static error verification. Herbie takes a floating-point expression, samples inputs to locate regimes where rounding error (especially catastrophic cancellation) is large against an arbitrary-precision MPFR reference, and uses algebraic rewrite rules in an e-graph together with series expansions and regime splitting (`if` branches over input ranges) to synthesize more accurate floating-point expressions. Daisy (building on Rosa) statically computes sound worst-case absolute and relative rounding error bounds for finite-precision numerical programs (across uniform or mixed float and fixed-point formats) by combining interval/affine range analysis with Taylor-form error propagation and SMT solving.

2. Eva Darulova and Viktor Kuncak (2014). "Sound Compilation of Reals." *Proceedings of the 41st ACM SIGPLAN-SIGACT Symposium on Principles of Programming Languages (POPL 2014)*, pp. 235-248. DOI: [10.1145/2535838.2535874](https://doi.org/10.1145/2535838.2535874) `[verified]`

DOI: [10.1007/978-3-319-89960-2_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-89960-2_15) `[verified]`

### How it resembles Luthier
Both lines of work share Luthier's principle `L13.1`: "code is written over ideal objects and realized by concrete ones; the realization's laws hold within a stated tolerance." Like Herbie, Luthier evaluates floating-point error against an exact or higher-precision reference (`decisions.md` proposes exact `Rational` arithmetic in the kernel via `WithinExact` so that `Float64` operations are tested against exact rationals without measurement error) and tracks error per input region (`concepts.md` section 8.1). Like Rosa and Daisy, Luthier separates the sensitivity (Lipschitz constant $L$) of the ideal mathematical function from the rounding defect ($\eta$) of the finite-precision realization on a bounded domain $D$ and radius $r$, composing them through the affine bound transformer $t_2 \circ t_1 = (L_2 L_1, L_2 \eta_1 + \eta_2, D_1 \cap f_1^{-1}(D_2), \min(r_1, (r_2 - \eta_1)/L_1))$ (`concepts.md` section 9.3, `D-2026-10-25-a`).

### How it differs
Herbie is an expression synthesizer driven by sampled error estimates without sound static guarantees, whereas Daisy is a static analyzer and compiler restricted to straight-line expressions and bounded loops whose operations Daisy knows how to analyze symbolically. Luthier is a specification and certification language for modular scientific programs, including external host routines in Python, C, or GPU libraries (`L11`). In Luthier, error bounds are explicit laws (`Within` and `WithinExact`, `BoundTransformer`) stated in theories (`numbers.lth`, `arrays.lth`), checked against negative controls (`noRoom`, `doublesThird`), and recorded in certificates with their worst observed distance-to-bound ratio (`concepts.md` sections 8.1 and 9.2).

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Regime splitting as domain-partitioned specimens:** Herbie improves accuracy by partitioning an input domain $D = D_1 \cup D_2$ and using one formula on $D_1$ (say, near zero) and another on $D_2$. In Luthier, two specimens certified on subdomains $D_1$ and $D_2$ can be combined into a piecewise specimen over $D_1 \cup D_2$.
2. **Subdivision for tighter static transformers:** Daisy subdivides input intervals when a global Lipschitz constant $L$ over $D$ is too loose (such as near a cancellation boundary). Luthier's static bound transformers (`core/properties/functions.lth`) could use domain subdivision to avoid over-pessimistic pull-backs when $L$ varies sharply across $D$.
3. **Mixed-precision tuning:** Daisy's mixed-precision assignment rules directly parallel the mixed-precision composition across `Floats (fmt : Format)` proposed in `decisions.md`, where conversions $\mathrm{fl}_B : F_A \to F_B$ are bound transformers with defect $u_B |x|$.

## 3. Arb and Ball Arithmetic, Interval Arithmetic, and Affine Arithmetic

### What it does
Rigorous enclosure methods represent real quantities together with guaranteed error bounds carried dynamically at runtime.
- **Interval arithmetic** (Moore 1966; IEEE Std 1788-2015) represents a quantity by an enclosure $[a, b]$ and rounds lower bounds downward and upper bounds upward at every operation. It is simple and universally sound, but suffers from the *dependency problem*: because $x - x$ treats the two occurrences of $x$ as independent, widths can grow exponentially (`[a, b] - [a, b] = [a - b, b - a]`).
- **Affine arithmetic** (Comba and Stolfi 1993; Stolfi and de Figueiredo 2003; de Figueiredo and Stolfi 2004) represents a quantity as a first-order polynomial $\hat{x} = x_0 + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \epsilon_i$ in noise symbols $\epsilon_i \in [-1, 1]$, tracking first-order correlations between intermediate variables so that $\hat{x} - \hat{x} = 0$, while accumulating nonlinear and rounding terms into new noise symbols.
- **Ball (midpoint-radius) arithmetic** (Johansson's Arb, 2017) represents an interval as $[m \pm \rho]$ where $m$ is an arbitrary-precision floating-point midpoint and $\rho$ is a compact, low-precision non-negative radius. At high precision, almost all work goes into the midpoint while radius propagation adds minimal overhead.

### How it resembles Luthier
`decisions.md` and `concepts.md` (sections 9.3, 10, and 15 `O5`) explicitly integrate ball arithmetic as the dynamic counterpart of Luthier's static bound transformers. By principle `L5.4` ("a decoration of the output is another model of the theory the code was written over"), `Ball` (midpoint $m$ and radius $\rho$) is another model of the generic number theory (`NumberModel`), allowing code written generically over numbers to run unchanged over balls. Moreover, `decisions.md` formulates the exact bridge between static and dynamic error propagation: propagating a ball through an operation is evaluating that operation's bound transformer $(L, \eta, D, r)$ at the actual midpoint $m$ to obtain the new radius $L(m)\rho + \eta(m)$, captured by the proposed law `RadiusWithinTransformer`.

### How it differs
Interval, ball, and affine arithmetic libraries are runtime data types: they cannot plan ahead before an input is known, cannot pull a demand's output tolerance $\varepsilon$ back to an input tolerance $\delta$ (`L6.8`), and cannot certify that an uninstrumented `Float64` or external LAPACK routine satisfies an error theorem. Luthier separates the *static* bound transformer (which is a priori, composes along the dependency cone, and enables the planner to choose a specimen or precision before execution) from the *dynamic* ball decoration (which computes a sharper point-wise radius at runtime and acts as the interface between concluded computations).

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **IEEE 1788 decorations for domain violations and branch straddling:** IEEE Std 1788 attaches a five-valued decoration (`com`, `dac`, `def`, `trv`, `ill`) to every interval to record whether every operation along its computation was continuous, defined, and single-valued on the enclosure. This directly addresses the branch-straddling problem noted in `decisions.md` ("a branch whose test a ball straddles has no bound").
2. **Short-precision radii from Arb:** As `decisions.md` notes, Arb represents the radius $\rho$ in a compact fixed-size format with directed upward rounding, keeping ball arithmetic fast while remaining rigorous.
3. **Noise-symbol sharing from affine arithmetic:** Where Luthier's static `transformSum` (`\sum_i L_i \delta_i + \eta`) overestimates error when two inputs to a binary operation descend from the same upstream node in the cone, affine arithmetic's noise symbols show how to cancel correlated first-order errors across a DAG.

## 4. Higham's and Goldberg's Floating-Point Error Analysis

### What it does
Classical forward and backward rounding error analysis provides the mathematical theorems that relate IEEE 754 floating-point computations to exact real arithmetic. Goldberg (1991) sistematizes IEEE 754 binary representations, ulps, guard digits, catastrophic cancellation, gradual underflow, and rounding modes. Higham (2002) develops the standard model $\mathrm{fl}(x \;\mathrm{op}\; y) = (x \;\mathrm{op}\; y)(1 + \delta)$ with $|\delta| \le u$, introduces the accumulated error constants $\gamma_n = nu / (1 - nu)$ (Lemma 3.1), and organizes numerical linear algebra around the triad of forward error, backward error (where the computed output $\hat{y}$ is the exact output for perturbed inputs $x + \Delta x$), and condition number $\kappa$, connected by $\text{forward error} \le \kappa \times \text{backward error}$. Rigal and Gaches (1967) establish the exact normwise backward error formula for linear systems $\lVert A\hat{y} - b \rVert / (\lVert A \rVert \lVert \hat{y} \rVert + \lVert b \rVert)$, and Muller et al. (2018) formalize parameterized floating-point formats $(p, e_{\min}, e_{\max})$.

Birkhäuser, Cham. DOI: [10.1007/978-3-319-76526-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-76526-6) `[verified]`

### How it resembles Luthier
Luthier directly turns Higham's, Goldberg's, Rigal and Gaches's, and Muller et al.'s theorems into executable, certifiable laws in `numbers.lth` and `arrays.lth` (`concepts.md` section 9, `decisions.md`). Specifically:
- `numbers.lth` and `arrays.lth` state bounds using Higham's $\gamma_n = nu/(1 - nu)$ (such as `AssociativeWithin` at $3\gamma_2(|x| + |y| + |z|)$ and `DotStable` at $3\gamma_n \sum_k |x_k y_k|$).
- `decisions.md` parameterizes floating-point theories by Muller et al.'s triple `Format = { p, emin, emax }` so that `Float64`, `Float32`, `Float16`, `BFloat16`, and `fp8` share one set of generic laws.
- `decisions.md` maps Higham's triad onto Luthier's bound transformer: forward error at exact inputs is the defect $\eta$ (stability law), condition number is the sensitivity $L$ (sensitivity law), backward error is a third law form (using Rigal and Gaches's residual for linear algebra), and "forward $\le$ condition $\times$ backward" is simply `ComposeSound` (`functions.lth`) applied to the backward error as input distance.

### How it differs
In the numerical analysis literature, these bounds live on paper in textbooks and comments, disconnected from the code that claims to satisfy them; if a library implementation deviates (for example, a compiler flag reassociates a Kahan sum, or a GPU unit truncates instead of rounding to nearest even), nothing in the program checks the theorem. Luthier encodes the bounds as `Within` and `WithinExact` laws, tests implementations against them over structured regions, records the empirical tightness ratio (`distance / bound`), and checks that tightening the bound below the theoretical threshold (`doublesThird`) is refuted by a witness (`(1, 2^{-53}, 2^{-53})`).

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Probabilistic error bounds (`\tilde{\gamma}_n \approx c \sqrt{n} u`):** Higham (2002, section 4.5) and Higham and Mary (2019) show that under mild stochastic assumptions on rounding errors, worst-case $\gamma_n = O(nu)$ shrinks to $O(\sqrt{n}u)$ with high probability. This fits Luthier's proposed clause `L13.6` (`decisions.md`), where a transformer's defect carries a risk parameter $\delta$ composed in the evidence base.
2. **Componentwise vs normwise backward error:** Higham distinguishes normwise backward error (Rigal and Gaches) from Oettli-Prager componentwise backward error ($|A\hat{y} - b| \le \varepsilon (|A||\hat{y}| + |b|)$). Offering both in `arrays.lth` would allow tighter certification for badly scaled matrices.

## 5. F\*, Dafny, Lean, and Coq/Rocq (Proof-Oriented Programming)

### What it does
Proof-oriented programming languages and interactive theorem provers express specifications as types or logical propositions and verify that implementations satisfy them via deductive proof:
- **F\*** combines dependent and refinement types (`{ x : t | p }`) with a lattice of monadic effects (`Pure`, `Ghost`, `ST`, `Exn`), discharging verification conditions through an SMT solver (Z3) or interactive tactic scripts, and recently exploring AI agent workflows that generate F\* specifications and proofs (`Ioannidis et al. 2026`).
- **Dafny** integrates pre/postconditions (`requires`, `ensures`), loop invariants, frame specifications, and ghost state into an imperative and functional language verified automatically via Boogie and Z3.
- **Lean 4** and **Coq/Rocq** are interactive theorem provers based on dependent type theory (the Calculus of Inductive Constructions) with small trusted kernels following Milner's LCF architecture, supporting both mathematical formalization (`mathlib`, `Flocq` for IEEE 754 floating-point proofs) and verified functional programming.

### How it resembles Luthier
Luthier explicitly takes type theory as its guide (`G0`) and uses refinement types `{ x : T | p }` as the equalizer of a law and `true` (`L8.5`, `L8.10`, `concepts.md` section 2.1). Its treatment of equality per object (`Object A` as a setoid in `equality.lth`, `L3.1`, `concepts.md` section 3.1), type-directed resolution of algebraic structures (`spec 4.R13`, section 4, analogous to Lean and Coq type classes), effect isolation between pure definitions in `U` and world actions on `W` (section 11, analogous to F\*'s `Pure`/`Ghost` vs effectful computations), and its LCF-style encapsulation of `Certificate` construction (`L10.10`, section 8.3, explicitly citing Milner's LCF) all come from this tradition. Furthermore, Flocq (`Boldo and Melquiond 2011`) formalizes floating-point formats and rounding in the same parameterized manner `(p, emin, emax)` that `decisions.md` adopts for `Floats (fmt : Format)`.

### How it differs
In F\*, Dafny, Lean, and Coq/Rocq, verification is binary and deductive: a proposition is either proved (or discharged by an SMT solver) or an unverified `admit`/`assume`/`sorry`. Because full formal proof of floating-point numerical libraries (such as SciPy or LAPACK) and stochastic simulations is rarely practical for working scientists, deductive systems cannot certify most existing scientific workflows. Luthier decouples the *law* (the proposition) from the *certifier* (`L9.1`, section 6.1), treating `Proved`, `Exhaustive`, `Cases`, `Sampled`, and `Assumed` as graded forms of evidence over a domain (`L10.2`), and allows foreign host functions (`extern`) to be certified empirically as models of a theory.

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **External deductive proof certificates for `Proved` grade:** Today `Proved` in Luthier cites a theorem or library guarantee (`spec 6.R1, 6.S9`). Luthier could allow a `Proved` certifier to link by content hash to a machine-checked Lean 4, Coq/Flocq, or SMT proof artifact for core laws like `BoundTransformer` (`ComposeSound`) or `Doubles` bounds.
2. **Audit rubrics and templates for AI specification synthesis:** F\*'s recent work on agentic proof-oriented programming (`Ioannidis et al. 2026`, `PoP-ART`) shows that AI agents proposing specifications must be constrained by structural rubrics to prevent trivial or circular specifications. This directly reinforces Luthier's vacuity checks (`6.6`), characterization rule (`spec 4.R10`), and negative controls (`7`) for the "Luthierizing a library" application (`applications-2026-10-27.md`).

## 6. QuickCheck, Hypothesis, Property-Based Testing, and Mutation Testing

### What it does
Property-based testing (PBT), introduced by QuickCheck for Haskell and extended with coverage-guided, stateful shrinking and database replay by Hypothesis for Python, checks universal propositions (`forall x, P(x)`) by generating random or structured test inputs. Because random testing can pass vacuously when a precondition `A ==> B` rarely holds, PBT tools track precondition satisfaction and input classifications (`classify`, `cover`), an issue closely related to vacuity detection in model checking (`Beer et al. 2001`). Mutation testing (`DeMillo, Lipton, and Sayward 1978`; `Jia and Harman 2011`) evaluates the adequacy of a test suite by injecting syntactic faults (*mutants*) into a program and measuring whether the tests kill (refute) each mutant. FitSpec (`Braquehais and Runciman 2016`) turns mutation testing around to evaluate *property sets* in QuickCheck: it generates mutant implementations of the functions under test and reports a property set as incomplete if any mutant survives all properties, or redundant if removing a property kills the exact same mutants.

Second, QuickCheck and Hypothesis treat a test run as an ephemeral pass/fail event in CI, whereas Luthier persists structured certificates keyed by the cone hash, pools samples across seeds (`spec 7.S13`), and converts sample counts into composable $(\hat\varepsilon, \delta)$ evidence costs (`L10.3`). ### What Luthier could take from it

2. **Integrated shrinking on refutation:** Hypothesis's internal representation of random choices as a byte stream enables automatic shrinking across complex dependent generators without user-written shrinkers. Adding byte-stream-backed shrinking to `Gen T` would produce minimal counterexamples both when a law fails on a model and when a control refutes a wrong model.

## 7. Proof-Carrying Code and Proof-Carrying Data

### What it does
- **Proof-Carrying Code (PCC)** (`Necula 1997`; `Appel 2001`) is a mechanism by which a code producer packages a compiled program binary together with a formal proof that the code satisfies a safety policy stipulated by the code consumer. The consumer runs a small, fast proof checker before executing the binary, removing the compiler from the trusted computing base.
- **Proof-Carrying Data (PCD)** (`Chiesa and Tromer 2010`; `Bitansky et al. 2013`) generalizes PCC from static programs to dynamic values flowing through a distributed computation DAG. Each node takes input messages carrying succinct compliance proofs, executes a local transition, and produces an output message with a recursively composed proof certifying that the entire distributed computation history leading to that datum satisfied a fixed local compliance predicate $\Pi$.

### How it differs

2. **Explicit trusted computing base minimization from Foundational PCC:** Appel's FPCC separates the small trusted kernel from untrusted external decision procedures. ## 8. Provenance (W3C PROV, RO-Crate, and Workflow Provenance)

### What it does
Data and workflow provenance systems record the lineage of computational results: which data inputs, software versions, parameters, and execution steps produced an output artifact.
- **W3C PROV** (`Moreau and Missier 2013`; `Moreau et al. 2015`) standardizes a domain-independent graph model of provenance built around three node types (`Entity`, `Activity`, `Agent`) and directed relations such as `wasGeneratedBy`, `used`, `wasDerivedFrom`, and `wasAttributedTo`.
- **RO-Crate** and **Workflow Run RO-Crate** (`Ó Carragáin et al. 2022`; `Leo et al. 2024`) package research outputs together with machine-readable JSON-LD metadata (following Schema.org and W3C PROV) describing prospective workflow definitions and retrospective execution traces across workflow engines (Nextflow, CWL, Galaxy, Snakemake).
- **Language-based provenance** (`Freire et al. 2008`; `Cheney, Acar, and Ahmed 2011`) studies provenance inside programming languages, showing semantically that provenance is a form of dependency analysis over execution traces.

## 9. Content-Addressed Code and Build Systems (Unison, Nix, Bazel)

### What it does
Content-addressed languages and build systems identify code definitions, packages, and build outputs by cryptographic hashes of their transitive dependencies rather than by mutable file paths or version strings:
- **Unison** represents each function and type definition by a 512-bit SHA3 hash of its syntax tree in a canonical form where variable names are replaced by positional de Bruijn indices and references to other definitions are replaced by their hashes. Renaming a definition changes zero hashes; pure test results keyed by the definition's hash are cached permanently and never re-run unless the cone changes.
- **Nix** (`Dolstra et al. 2004, 2008`) stores every software component in `/nix/store/<hash>-<name>`, where the hash is computed over the complete transitive dependency graph of build inputs, build scripts, and environment variables.
- **Bazel** and the **Build Systems à la Carte** framework (`Mokhov, Mitchell, and Peyton Jones 2018, 2020`) formalize build systems along two axes: scheduling (topological vs suspending/demand-driven) and rebuild checking (dirty bits vs input-addressed cryptographic hashes vs constructive traces that cache targets across machines).

### How it resembles Luthier
Luthier's dependency cone hashing (`concepts.md` section 8.2, `L10.4`, `spec 6.S8`) and pure result memoization (`concepts.md` section 12.4, `L5.6`, `spec 8.S8`) combine Unison's canonical AST hashing with Nix's external environment hashing and Bazel's constructive traces:
- Like Unison, Luthier hashes the transitive dependency cone of a definition in canonical form (`canon(cone(n))`, with bound variables alpha-normalized, module prefixes removed, and binder type annotations erased, `L3.4`), so alpha-equivalent rewrites or local renamings do not invalidate certificates.
- Like Unison's cached pure tests, Luthier's principle `L4.7` ("a definition's result depends only on its inputs") guarantees that a certificate is valid for as long as its cone hash is unchanged (`spec 12.S11`).
- Like Nix, Luthier includes the external host code behind `extern` calls and the interpreter revision in the cone hash (`H(canon(cone(n)) || hosts || interpreter)`), and includes kernel changes by content hash (`D-2026-10-27-a`).
- In Mokhov et al.'s taxonomy, Luthier's `run` and `certify` stores are demand-driven suspending systems with constructive traces.

### How it differs
Unison hashes only pure Unison terms and treats tests as Boolean pass/fail properties; Nix and Bazel hash build inputs to cache compiled artifacts or byte outputs without any notion of mathematical theories, statistical sampling distributions, or numerical tolerances. ### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Hash-based dependency pinning for `hosts` via Nix derivations:** Hashing a Python function's bytecode or AST alone can miss changes in transitive C/Fortran shared libraries (such as MKL vs OpenBLAS). Allowing the `hosts` component of a cone hash to incorporate a Nix store path hash or lockfile digest when available would make host extern hashes hermetic down to the BLAS/LAPACK binary.
2. **Early cutoff from Build Systems à la Carte:** Mokhov et al. formalize *early cutoff* (when an upstream input changes, but an intermediate step produces the exact same output or normal form, downstream targets need not rebuild). In Luthier, if a refactoring changes a file's text or helper names without changing `canon(cone(n))`, early cutoff explains why certificates and downstream result keys remain valid.

## 10. E-Graphs, Equality Saturation (egg), and AC-Normalization in Term Rewriting

### What it does
Term rewriting and equality saturation address how to decide or search equational equivalence classes of symbolic expressions:
- **Term rewriting and AC-normalization** (`Dershowitz and Jouannaud 1990`; `Baader and Nipkow 1998`) compute canonical normal forms modulo an equational theory $E$. When $E$ consists of associativity and commutativity (AC), canonicalization flattens nested applications of associative operators and sorts arguments of commutative operators under a total term ordering (such as a lexicographic path ordering). For more general equations, Knuth-Bendix completion attempts to orient equations into a confluent, terminating rewrite system, and **higher-order pattern unification** (`Miller 1991`) decides matching of $\beta\delta$-normal $\lambda$-terms when free pattern variables are applied only to distinct bound variables.
- **E-graphs and equality saturation** (`Tate et al. 2009`; `Willsey et al. 2021`, `egg`) avoid the need for a terminating, confluent normal form when rewrite rules are bidirectional or non-terminating (such as $a + b = b + a$ or $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$). An e-graph compactly represents an exponentially large set of equivalent terms grouped into e-classes; equality saturation applies all rewrite rules simultaneously until saturation (or a timeout) and extracts the optimal term from an e-class according to a cost function.

1. **Miller pattern matching + AC-canonicalization for `check` notices and control reuse:** Implementing $\beta\delta\alpha$-normalization plus argument permutation and AC-sorting for certified exact operators (`==`, `and`, `or`, integer `+`, float `+` for commutativity only) on Miller's pattern fragment is decidable, fast, and suffices to recognize `ReadingSymmetric` as `Commutative`, automatically sharing the core's control templates and certificates (`idea-law-normal-form-2026-10-27.md`).
2. **Weighted / metric e-graphs for the planner:** Rather than merging metric specimens into equivalence classes, Luthier's planner (`bite E1`) can represent exact Boolean identities as e-classes (using `egg`'s union-find) and metric identities as weighted directed edges between e-classes annotated with bound transformers $(L, \eta, D, r)$ and evidence costs $(\hat\varepsilon, \delta)$, reducing planner search to constrained shortest-path extraction over an e-graph.

## 11. Graded and Quantitative Type Theories (Granule, Fuzz) and Lawvere Metric Spaces

### What it does
Enriched category theory and graded/quantitative type systems generalize logic and types from qualitative Booleans to quantitative algebraic structures:
- **Lawvere metric spaces** (`Lawvere 1973`) observe that a generalized metric space is a category enriched over the symmetric monoidal closed category $([0, \infty], \ge, +, 0)$, just as a preordered set (or equivalence relation) is a category enriched over $(\mathrm{Bool}, \implies, \land, \mathrm{true})$, and a **probabilistic metric space** (`Menger 1942`) is enriched over distance distribution functions under a t-norm.
- **Fuzz** (`Reed and Pierce 2010`) is a linear type system indexed by real sensitivities $c \in [0, \infty]$ in which a function type $A \multimap_c B$ means the function is $c$-Lipschitz ($d_B(f(x), f(y)) \le c \cdot d_A(x, y)$), with additive product $A \otimes B$ summing input sensitivities and comonadic scaling $!_c A$ multiplying sensitivities to certify differential privacy.
- **Quantitative Type Theory (QTT)** (`McBride 2016`; `Atkey 2018`) and **Granule** (`Orchard, Liepelt, and Eades 2019`) annotate variable bindings and graded modal boxes $\Box_r A$ with elements $r$ of an arbitrary partially ordered semiring (or resource algebra), tracking usage counts, sensitivities, privacy budgets, or security levels inside the type checker.

This literature is the direct mathematical foundation of Luthier's core (`concepts.md` sections 3.2, 9.3, and 10, `G2`, `L3.5`, `L6.15`, `D-2026-10-18-c`, `D-2026-10-25-a`):
- `concepts.md` section 3.2 explicitly cites Lawvere (1973) and Menger (1942) for Luthier's three levels of equivalence: `Bool` with `and` (equivalence), $[0, \infty]$ with $+$ and $\ge$ (`ErrorBase`, metric space), and distributions of distances with a t-norm (probabilistic metric space).
- `concepts.md` section 9.3 explicitly notes that the sum of sensitivities over multiple inputs in Luthier's bound transformer ($\varepsilon \le \sum_i L_i \delta_i + \eta$, `transformSum`) is the tensor product $A \otimes B$ of Reed and Pierce's Fuzz (`transformMax` corresponding to the cartesian product $A \mathbin{\&} B$).
- Like Granule, Luthier separates different quantitative dimensions into distinct ordered algebraic bases (`ErrorBase`, `EvidenceComposition`, `Resources` as a typed work/span/memory vector) rather than collapsing them into a single number (`concepts.md` section 10).

### How it differs
In Fuzz, Granule, and QTT, every sensitivity $c$ is a purely multiplicative Lipschitz constant ($d(f(x), f(y)) \le c \cdot d(x, y)$, so $x = y \implies f(x) = f(y)$) tracked statically by linear/graded typing rules on the syntax of a single closed functional language. As `concepts.md` section 9.1 (`L13.3`, `L13.4`) observes, floating-point rounding and numerical discretizations are *not* Lipschitz: two reals arbitrarily close across a rounding boundary land one ulp apart. Therefore, Luthier must replace Fuzz's pure Lipschitz constant $L$ with the four-tuple affine bound transformer $(L, \eta, D, r)$ that carries an additive defect $\eta > 0$, a validity domain $D$, and an input radius $r$ (`D-2026-10-25-a`), and must allow $L$ and $\eta$ to be certified either by theorem or empirically over `extern` implementations.

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Fuzz's tensor vs cartesian product distinction for multi-argument laws:** Fuzz distinguishes $f : A \otimes B \multimap C$ (errors sum: $\delta_A + \delta_B$, Luthier's `transformSum`) from $f : A \mathbin{\&} B \multimap C$ (errors take max: $\max(\delta_A, \delta_B)$, Luthier's `transformMax`). Using this distinction in the planner's pull-back (`L6.2`, `L6.8`) gives a principled rule for splitting an output tolerance $\varepsilon$ across multiple arguments: under `transformMax`, each argument receives the full $(\varepsilon - \eta)/L_i$ without having to divide the error budget across inputs.
2. **Graded comonadic modality for `WithCertificate` / `WithDerivation` (`O5`, `O13`):** Granule's graded modal type $\Box_r A$ provides a clean type-theoretic account of values indexed by a grade or derivation summary $r$, where the counit $\mathrm{extract} : \Box_r A \to A$ is Luthier's forgetful projection (`L8.5`, `L5.23`) and $\mathrm{extend}$ composes grades along a computation DAG.

## 12. Sequential Testing and Confidence Sequences (Wald; Howard, Ramdas et al.)

### What it does
Sequential hypothesis testing and time-uniform confidence sequences provide valid statistical inference when sample sizes are not fixed in advance or when samples accumulate incrementally over time:
- **Rule of three** (`Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983`) derives the exact binomial confidence upper bound $p \le 1 - \delta^{1/n} \approx -\ln(\delta)/n$ ($\approx 3/n$ at $\delta = 0.05$) for the failure probability $p$ when $n$ independent trials produce zero failures.
- **Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)** (`Wald 1945, 1947`) tests a null hypothesis $H_0 : p \le p_0$ against an alternative $H_1 : p \ge p_1$ sequentially after each observation, stopping as soon as the likelihood ratio crosses an upper threshold (reject $H_0$) or lower threshold (accept $H_0$) calculated from target type-I and type-II error rates $(\alpha, \beta)$. **Statistical Model Checking (SMC)** (`Sen, Viswanathan, and Agha 2004`; `Younes and Simmons 2006`) applies Wald's SPRT to verify formal specification formulas over stochastic systems.
- **Confidence sequences and testing by betting** (`Howard, Ramdas, McAuliffe, and Sekhon 2021`; `Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2024`) construct nonasymptotic, nonparametric *time-uniform* confidence bounds $\mathbb{P}(\forall n \ge 1,\; p \in C_n(\delta)) \ge 1 - \delta$ using supermartingales (e-processes), remaining statistically valid under arbitrary optional stopping, adaptive continuation, and pooling of sample stretches.

### How it resembles Luthier

### How it differs
In classical fixed-sample binomial bounds (such as Hanley and Lippman-Hand's rule of three), the sample size $n$ must be fixed in advance: if a user runs $n_1 = 200$ cases, inspects $\hat\varepsilon$, finds that a new demand requires a tighter $\hat\varepsilon$, and runs an additional $n_2 = 800$ cases under a new seed to pool into $n = 1000$ (`D-2026-10-16-g`, `spec 7.S13`), fixed-$n$ confidence intervals technically suffer from optional-continuation inflation unless adjusted for sequential peeking. Conversely, confidence sequences (`Howard et al. 2021`; `Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2024`) are designed specifically for anytime-valid sequential pooling, but have not previously been integrated into a programming language's certificate store or type-level cost algebra.

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Anytime-valid confidence sequences for sample pooling (`spec 7.S13`):** Because Luthier explicitly pools runs across seeds as a growing sample (`concepts.md` section 6.4, `D-2026-10-16-g`) and computes $\hat\varepsilon$ at serve time for whatever $\delta$ the demand supplies (bite B1), Howard et al. (2021) and Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2024) provide the exact mathematical justification and anytime-valid formula $\hat\varepsilon_{\text{seq}}(n, \delta)$ when runs are added adaptively until a demand's cutoff is met. Note: for zero failures ($k = 0$), a simple e-process or Ville's inequality gives an anytime-valid upper bound at no extra storage cost beyond $n$.
2. **E-values for composing evidence across laws:** E-values (non-negative random variables with $\mathbb{E}[E] \le 1$ under the null) multiply under independence or sequential conditioning and average under arbitrary dependence, offering a clean probabilistic companion to `EvidenceComposition` (`bite B2`).

## 13. LLM Routing, Model Selection by Cost, and Specification Synthesis

### What it does
As multiple large language models with different cost and capability profiles have become available via APIs, LLM routing and cascading systems dynamically choose which model to invoke per query:
- **FrugalGPT** (`Chen, Zaharia, and Zou 2024`) learns a generation scoring function and queries a cascade of LLMs from cheapest to most expensive, stopping as soon as the predicted quality score exceeds a threshold.
- **RouteLLM** (`Ong et al. 2025`) trains binary and matrix-factorization routers on human preference data (Chatbot Arena) to route easier queries to a weak, cheap model and harder queries to a strong model to optimize a cost-quality trade-off parameter.
- **AutoMix** (`Madaan et al. 2024`) uses few-shot self-verification and a POMDP router to decide whether a small model's output is trustworthy or needs escalation to a larger model.
- In formal methods, **Daikon** (`Ernst et al. 2001`) mines likely program invariants dynamically from execution traces, while recent work such as **Endres et al. (2024)** uses LLMs to synthesize formal postconditions from natural language and code, using test cases and mutation testing to validate the generated specifications.

### How it resembles Luthier
`applications-2026-10-27.md` describes two applications of Luthier's thesis: (1) "Luthierizing a library," where an AI agent proposes theories, laws, and wrong-model controls for an external scientific library and Luthier's tester refutes or certifies them (extending the invariant discovery and postcondition synthesis paradigm of `Ernst et al. 2001` and `Endres et al. 2024`), and (2) choosing among LLM providers by certified results per cost. In the second application, the task is a `Theory`, the language models (Claude, DeepSeek, GPT, local models) are the *specimens*, the evidence is the fraction of proposals certified by Luthier's tester (excluding vacuous and uncontrolled laws), and the planner selects the cheapest specimen that satisfies the demand's quality and jurisdiction constraints.

### How it differs
Existing LLM routers (`FrugalGPT`, `RouteLLM`, `AutoMix`) evaluate candidate models using generic static benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K), learned preference classifiers (Chatbot Arena), or unverified LLM self-confidence scores, which can be miscalibrated on specialized scientific code. In Luthier's formulation (`applications-2026-10-27.md`), the quality oracle is objective and task-specific: Luthier's own verifier (`luthier check` and `luthier certify`), together with vacuity rejection (`6.6`) and negative controls (`7`), measures each model's actual certification rate per dollar on the user's task, while data jurisdiction is handled as a domain constraint on the demand.

### What Luthier could take from it
1. **Cascading specimens in the planner:** FrugalGPT and AutoMix show that a cheap model can be tried first and escalated to a stronger model only when verification fails. Because Luthier has an exact mechanized verifier (`check` + `certify` with controls and vacuity checks), a cascade specimen `try_then(cheap_model, strong_model)` has zero false-positive risk whenever the verifier is sound, achieving lower expected API cost than static single-model selection.
2. **Mutant-killing evaluation metrics from Endres et al. (2024):** Endres et al. evaluate LLM-generated postconditions by measuring both *correctness* (does the reference implementation pass?) and *completeness* (what fraction of buggy mutants does the postcondition reject?). This is the exact metric needed in `applications-2026-10-27.md` to score LLM specimens on "Luthierizing a library."

## 14. Additional Work Closer to Specific Aspects of Luthier

The thirteen lines of work above leave out five bodies of research that are even closer to specific foundational pieces of Luthier. A paper or grant proposal on Luthier should cite them so reviewers from those communities see their work recognized.

### 14.1 Algebraic Specification, Institutions, and Computer Algebra Categories (CASL, Axiom/Aldor, FoCaLiZe)

- **What it does:** Goguen and Burstall's theory of *Institutions* (1992) formalizes the concept of a specification language in terms of signatures, sentences (axioms/laws), models (algebras interpreting the signature), and a satisfaction relation $\mathfrak{M} \models_\Sigma \varphi$ invariant under signature morphisms. In scientific and symbolic computation, **Axiom** and **Aldor** (`Jenks and Sutor 1992`) organized an entire computer algebra library around *categories* (theories with operations and mathematical axioms) and *domains* (models implementing those categories). **FOC / FoCaLiZe** (`Prevosto and Doligez 2002`) turned Axiom's unchecked axioms into formal theorems over *species* and *collections*, compiling implementations to OCaml and proof obligations to Coq and Zenon.
- **Key references:**
  1. Joseph A. Goguen and Rod M. Burstall (1992). "Institutions: Abstract Model Theory for Specification and Programming." *Journal of the ACM*, Vol. 39, No. 1, pp. 95-146. DOI: [10.1145/147508.147524](https://doi.org/10.1145/147508.147524) `[verified]`
  2. Richard D. Jenks and Robert S. Sutor (1992). *AXIOM: The Scientific Computation System*. Springer-Verlag, New York. DOI: [10.1007/978-1-4612-2940-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4612-2940-7) `[verified]`
  3. Virgile Prevosto and Damien Doligez (2002). "Algorithms and Proofs Inheritance in the FOC Language." *Journal of Automated Reasoning*, Vol. 29, No. 3-4, pp. 337-363. DOI: [10.1023/A:1021979218446](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1021979218446) `[verified]`
- **How it resembles Luthier:** This is the direct intellectual ancestor of Luthier's first organizing principle `G1` ("a type is a theory and its values are its models"), `D-2026-10-12-a` ("Luthier is presented as a verifier of models of theories, not of functions"), the three planes of the core (`concepts.md` section 3.3), and the algebraic hierarchy (`Object`, `Monoid`, `Metric`, `Doubles`) built from generic properties (`Commutative`, `Associative`).
- **How it differs:** Axiom left its category axioms as informal comments; FoCaLiZe required full deductive Coq proofs and dealt with exact symbolic algebra. Neither handled floating-point within-bound laws (`AssociativeWithin`, `BoundTransformer`), graded empirical certification (`Sampled`, `Cases`, controls), or demand-driven selection across external host libraries.
- **What Luthier could take from it:** Goguen and Burstall's *satisfaction condition* ($\mathfrak{M}' \models_\Sigma \sigma(\varphi) \iff \sigma^*(\mathfrak{M}') \models_{\Sigma'} \varphi$) gives the exact categorical statement for how Luthier transports certificates and controls along theory morphisms and field renamings (`L3.4`).

### 14.2 Quantitative Algebraic Reasoning (Mardare, Panangaden, and Plotkin)

- **What it does:** Mardare, Panangaden, and Plotkin (2016) generalize Birkhoff's equational logic and universal algebra from sets with equality ($s = t$) to metric spaces with quantitative equations ($s =_\varepsilon t$, read as "the distance between $s$ and $t$ is at most $\varepsilon$"). Their deduction rules replace transitivity with the archimedean triangle rule ($s =_\varepsilon t$ and $t =_{\varepsilon'} u$ entail $s =_{\varepsilon + \varepsilon'} u$) and require operations of a quantitative algebra to be Lipschitz / non-expansive so that input distances propagate to output distances.
- **Key reference:**
  1. Radu Mardare, Prakash Panangaden, and Gordon Plotkin (2016). "Quantitative Algebraic Reasoning." *Proceedings of the 31st Annual ACM/IEEE Symposium on Logic in Computer Science (LICS 2016)*, pp. 700-709. DOI: [10.1145/2933575.2934518](https://doi.org/10.1145/2933575.2934518) `[verified]`
- **How it resembles Luthier:** This paper is the closest theoretical counterpart to the union of Luthier's `G1` (theories and models) and `G2` (laws valued in cost/distance). Their indexed judgment $s =_\varepsilon t$, lack of unadorned transitivity, triangle composition $\varepsilon + \varepsilon'$, and quantitative models over metric spaces closely match Luthier's `Within` laws (`AssociativeWithin`, `MetricWithin`), `L12.3` ("two links each within $\varepsilon$ are within $2\varepsilon$"), and `concepts.md` sections 3.2, 9, and 13.
- **How it differs:** Quantitative algebras assume operations are strictly non-expansive or $L$-Lipschitz ($d(f(x), f(x)) = 0$) and develop deductive completeness theorems (Birkhoff-style variety theorems) for exact metric spaces, mainly targeting Markov processes and probabilistic programming. Luthier adds the additive realization defect $\eta$ required by floating-point rounding and discretizations (`BoundTransformer`), restricts laws to explicit validity domains $D$ and radii $r$ (`D-2026-10-18-j`), and certifies quantitative models empirically.
- **What Luthier could take from it:** Mardare, Panangaden, and Plotkin's deductive calculus for $=_\varepsilon$ provides a ready-made logical foundation for Paper 2 ("Quantitative scientific laws" in `gpt-dialogue.md`), showing how Luthier's `BoundTransformer` and `Within` laws extend quantitative equational logic with additive defects $\eta$ and bounded domains $(D, r)$.

### 14.3 Hybrid Verification, Gradual Verification, and Foundational PBT (Flanagan; Wise et al.; QuickChick)

- **What it does:** Hybrid type checking (`Flanagan 2006`) and gradual verification (`Wise et al. 2020`) bridge static deductive verification and dynamic contract checking: specifications that can be proved statically are discharged at compile time, while specifications that cannot be proved statically (or that cross into unverified code) are compiled into runtime checks. Conversely, **QuickChick** (`Paraskevopoulou et al. 2015`) integrates property-based testing into Coq, proving inside Coq itself that a random generator is sound and complete with respect to an inductive specification.
- **Key references:**
  1. Cormac Flanagan (2006). "Hybrid Type Checking." *Conference Record of the 33rd ACM SIGPLAN-SIGACT Symposium on Principles of Programming Languages (POPL 2006)*, pp. 245-256. DOI: [10.1145/1111037.1111059](https://doi.org/10.1145/1111037.1111059) `[verified]`
  2. Jenna Wise, Johannes Bader, Cameron Wong, Jonathan Aldrich, Éric Tanter, and Joshua Sunshine (2020). "Gradual Verification of Recursive Heap Data Structures." *Proceedings of the ACM on Programming Languages*, Vol. 4, Issue OOPSLA, Article 228. DOI: [10.1145/3428296](https://doi.org/10.1145/3428296) `[verified]`
  3. Zoe Paraskevopoulou, Catalin Hriţcu, Maxime Dénès, Leonidas Lampropoulos, and Benjamin C. Pierce (2015). "Foundational Property-Based Testing." *Interactive Theorem Proving (ITP 2015)*, Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Vol. 9236, pp. 325-343. DOI: [10.1007/978-3-319-22102-1_22](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-319-22102-1_22) `[verified]`
- **How it resembles Luthier:** Like hybrid and gradual verification, Luthier allows a program to combine specifications backed by different strengths of evidence (`Proved`, `Exhaustive`, `Cases`, `Sampled`, `Assumed`, `L9.1`, `L10.2`), and checks host actions dynamically at their first application (`spec 10.S10`). Like QuickChick, Luthier treats generators themselves as mathematical objects that satisfy certified laws (`spec 7.S1`, `concepts.md` section 6.2).
- **How it differs:** Gradual verification inserts per-call runtime assertion checks during production runs whenever a static proof is missing. Luthier instead performs ahead-of-time certification (`luthier certify`) stored in `x.cert.json` with explicit domain and $(\hat\varepsilon, \delta)$ evidence grades, and `luthier run` checks the certificate store before executing rather than slowing down inner numerical loops with runtime assertions.
- **What Luthier could take from it:** QuickChick's formal definition of generator *soundness* (every generated value satisfies the subtype predicate) and *completeness* (every region/value in the subtype has non-zero probability mass of being generated) directly sharpens Luthier's generator laws (`spec 7.S1`) and region-coverage rules (`L9.16`).

### 14.4 Reliability- and Accuracy-Aware Approximate Computing (Chisel)

- **What it does:** Chisel (`Misailovic et al. 2014`) is an optimization framework for approximate computing that takes a kernel together with a quantitative accuracy specification (maximum allowable output error) and a probabilistic reliability specification (probability $1 - \delta$ that the output satisfies the accuracy bound), and uses sensitivity analysis and integer linear programming to select approximate operations and data representations that minimize energy/execution cost while respecting both bounds.
- **Key reference:**
  1. Sasa Misailovic, Michael Carbin, Sara Achour, Zichao Qi, and Martin C. Rinard (2014). "Chisel: Reliability- and Accuracy-Aware Optimization of Approximate Computational Kernels." *Proceedings of the 2014 ACM International Conference on Object Oriented Programming Systems Languages & Applications (OOPSLA 2014)*, pp. 309-328. DOI: [10.1145/2660193.2660231](https://doi.org/10.1145/2660193.2660231) `[verified]`
- **How it resembles Luthier:** Chisel combines Lipschitz sensitivity propagation, additive error bounds, and probabilistic failure bounds (composed by Boole's union bound) to select lower-cost realizations that satisfy a caller's accuracy and reliability demand. This is remarkably close to Luthier's separation of the error base $([0, \infty], +, \ge)$ and the evidence/reliability base $(\hat\varepsilon, \delta)$ under the capped sum (`concepts.md` section 10, `D-2026-10-25-a`), and to clause `L13.6` in `decisions.md`.
- **How it differs:** Chisel analyzes hardware fault injection and approximate arithmetic instructions inside C kernels using an external specification file. It has no algebraic theories, no models or negative controls, no content-addressed certificate store, and no value derivations across language boundaries.
- **What Luthier could take from it:** Chisel's ILP formulation for allocating error and failure budgets $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ across the nodes of a computation DAG solves the exact budget-splitting problem noted in `concepts.md` section 9.3 (`L6.2`: "How a demand's $\varepsilon$ is split among several inputs is not determined by the bound").

### 14.5 Uniqueness Typing for World Actions (Clean)

- **What it does:** Clean (`Barendsen and Smetsers 1996`) is a purely functional graph-rewriting language that handles I/O and destructive array updates without monads by passing a unique `*World` environment value. The uniqueness type system guarantees statically that a value of type `*World` has a single reference and is never duplicated, so side-effecting operations (`*World -> (*World, a)`) are serialized along a linear chain of data dependencies inside a pure functional graph.
- **Key reference:**
  1. Erik Barendsen and Sjaak Smetsers (1996). "Uniqueness Typing for Functional Languages with Graph Rewriting Semantics." *Mathematical Structures in Computer Science*, Vol. 6, No. 6, pp. 579-612. DOI: [10.1017/S0960129500070109](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0960129500070109) `[verified]`
- **How it resembles Luthier:** `concepts.md` section 11 explicitly cites Clean's uniqueness types (`D-2026-10-13-b`, `L4.11`): Luthier's world primitive `W` enters at `main`, has no `==`, is used once per action (`W -> ... -> W * A`), and makes the ordering of side effects an ordinary dependency edge in the program DAG (`L4.1`).
- **How it differs:** Clean treats external I/O functions on `*World` as trusted primitives, whereas Luthier requires actions on `W` to be certified against a theory of the world (`world.lth`, observing the world before and after where readable, `L4.9`, `L4.10`, and validating host actions at first application, `spec 10.S10`).
- **What Luthier could take from it:** Clean's sub-world splitting (`openfiles : *World -> (*Files, *World)`) allows independent parts of the world to be acted on in parallel without false serialization edges, which directly implements Luthier's `D-2026-10-13-a` and `L4.12` ("whether two parts of the world are independent is a theory in a library").

## 15. Where Luthier's Combination Is New and Where It Is Not

### Where it is not new
Taken individually, almost every building block of Luthier has a substantial literature behind it:
1. **Types as theories and values as models (`G1`, sections 2-4):** Algebraic specifications with axioms and models go back to Goguen and Burstall's Institutions (1992), Axiom/Aldor's categories and domains (1992), FoCaLiZe's species and collections (2002), and dependent record / type class hierarchies in Coq, Lean, and Haskell.
2. **Subtypes carved out by predicates (`L8.5`, `L8.10`):** Refinement types `{ x : T | p }` are standard in PVS, Liquid Haskell, F\*, and Dafny.
3. **Random testing of laws, precondition tracking, and mutant refutation (sections 6-7):** Property-based testing with explicit generators comes from QuickCheck (2000) and Hypothesis (2019); vacuity detection comes from model checking (`Beer et al. 2001`); and testing a specification by checking that it refutes wrong implementations (Luthier's controls, `L9.14`) was demonstrated for functional property sets by FitSpec (`Braquehais and Runciman 2016`).
4. **Statistical bounds for zero failures and sequential testing (section 6.4):** The rule of three (`Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983`), Wald's SPRT (`Wald 1945`), Statistical Model Checking (`Younes and Simmons 2006`), and time-uniform confidence sequences (`Howard et al. 2021`) are established in statistics and formal verification.
5. **Floating-point error bounds and dynamic enclosures (section 9, `decisions.md`):** Forward/backward error and $\gamma_n$ bounds are textbook numerical analysis (`Goldberg 1991`; `Higham 2002`; `Rigal and Gaches 1967`); static propagation via affine sensitivity and rounding defects was built in Rosa/Daisy (`Darulova and Kuncak 2014, 2018`); and dynamic midpoint-radius ball arithmetic is implemented in Arb (`Johansson 2017`).
6. **Quantitative logics and Lawvere metric enrichment (sections 3.2, 10):** Enriching equivalence into $[0, \infty]$ and distance distributions comes from Lawvere (1973) and Menger (1942); sensitivity typing comes from Fuzz (`Reed and Pierce 2010`) and Granule (`Orchard et al. 2019`); and equational theories indexed by metric tolerances $=_\varepsilon$ were formalized by Mardare, Panangaden, and Plotkin (2016).
7. **Content-addressed cones and world tokens (sections 8.2, 11):** Hashing canonical AST cones comes from Unison and Nix; single-use world tokens come from Clean (`Barendsen and Smetsers 1996`).
8. **Algorithmic choice under accuracy targets (section 13):** PetaBricks (`Ansel et al. 2009, 2011`) and Chisel (`Misailovic et al. 2014`) already select implementations based on accuracy and resource trade-offs.

A paper or grant on Luthier should state plainly that it does not invent any of these ingredients in isolation.

### Where the combination is new
What does not appear in prior systems is the architectural unification of these ingredients around a single semantic pipeline for scientific software:
1. **Unifying deductive proof, exhaustive enumeration, finite case lists, and statistical sampling as graded evidence over a domain (`L9.1`, `L10.2`, `D-2026-10-25-a`):** Proof assistants (F\*, Lean, Coq, FoCaLiZe) require deductive proofs; PBT tools (QuickCheck, Hypothesis) run ephemeral tests without persistent, composable evidence bounds. Luthier separates the mathematical law from the certifier and represents the strength of any certificate as a pair `(domain, evidence cost)` where `Cases` and `Sampled` are incomparable (a `Cases` certificate serving only listed inputs, a `Sampled` certificate serving the sampled distribution with $(\hat\varepsilon, \delta)$ composed under the union bound).
2. **Making conjunctive negative controls (`L9.14`) and vacuity rejection (`Vacuous n`) part of the persisted certificate of a theory:** FitSpec used mutants as an external developer utility; Luthier makes refuting a wrong model per conjunct and verifying non-vacuous premise coverage structural requirements for a theory to be reported as controlled in `check -v`, separating evidence about the *model* (passes) from evidence about the *law* (controls, `concepts.md` section 8.4).
3. **Joining static affine bound transformers with dynamic ball arithmetic inside quantitative theories (`decisions.md`, `concepts.md` section 9.3):** Fuzz and quantitative algebras assume $L$-Lipschitz operations with zero defect at equal inputs; Daisy and Arb are separate static and dynamic tools. Luthier formulates numerical laws as affine bound transformers $(L, \eta, D, r)$ that both pull a demand's tolerance $\varepsilon$ back statically for planning and evaluate point-wise at runtime as a `Ball` model of the same theory (`RadiusWithinTransformer`).
4. **Demand-driven realization choice constrained by certified non-transitive identities (`L6`, `L10.7`, sections 12-13):** Unlike PetaBricks (which autotunes over uncertified alternatives), Luthier restricts implementation substitution to paths in a graph of certified identities whose accumulated metric distance and evidence cost remain within the demand's cutoffs.
5. **Typed value derivations across language boundaries (`L5.22`, `L5.23`, `L10.13`, `L11.10`):** Provenance standards (W3C PROV, RO-Crate) record passive logs outside the language; Proof-Carrying Data (`Chiesa and Tromer 2010`) proves cryptographic compliance with a fixed Boolean policy. Luthier distinguishes the certificate of a model, the observation of one run, and the derivation of a value, allowing a demand to project and constrain the semantically relevant history (models, error bounds, evidence grades, bridges, and roots of trust) of values that cross between Python, Julia, C, and Luthier.

### What remains to be built or proved for that claim to hold in full
As `concepts.md` section 15 and `decisions.md` record, the core (`v0.3`), three-file separation, cone-keyed certificate stores, vacuity tracking, per-conjunct controls, and the Boolean/metric slices of `BoundTransformer` and `EvidenceComposition` are implemented, while four parts of the combination are still in progress (`[building]` or `[proposed]`):
- closing `O1` (wiring the cost types `C` into certification admission, completing `Resources` and `Uncertainty`);
- building the planner and its realization graph (`wave E`, currently bound to a single specimen per name);
- implementing `Rational` kernel arithmetic (`WithinExact`) and `Ball` decorations (`O5`) as proposed in `decisions.md`;
- settling the concrete representation and cross-language bridge declarations for value derivations (`O13`).
